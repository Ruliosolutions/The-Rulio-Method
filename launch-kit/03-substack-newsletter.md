# Rulio Substack Newsletter — Setup + First 3 Issues

> Founder-led. Honest. Five-minute reads about solfeggio, focus, and what
> the research actually says. This file contains everything you need to
> launch the Rulio newsletter: setup steps, publication metadata, the
> welcome email, and the first three issues written and ready to paste.

## Setup steps for the user

Before you publish anything, work through these in order. Most of them
take less than ten minutes.

1. **Sign up at substack.com.** Use the founder email
   (`roel@rulio.app` or whatever you already reply from). Substack
   doesn't care about a custom domain at first — you can add one later.
2. **Create the publication** with the suggested name below
   ("The Rulio Method" is the one I recommend — see why in the
   publication setup section).
3. **Set the bio and tagline** exactly as written below. Substack has
   two fields: "Publication name" and "Subtitle." Treat the Subtitle as
   your tagline and the long-form bio block as your "About" page.
4. **Enable the welcome email.** In Substack Settings → Welcome Email,
   paste the text below. This is the email that auto-sends the moment
   someone subscribes. It is the highest-converting email you will ever
   send — treat it like one.
5. **Publish Issue 1** as a normal post on launch day. Don't schedule
   the first one — you want it live, on your site, shareable the same
   hour it goes out.
6. **Schedule Issues 2 and 3** for the next two weeks, one per week
   (Tuesday or Wednesday, 8:00 AM Brussels time, tends to land best for
   the EU + US-East overlap).
7. **Share each issue on Twitter and LinkedIn the day it publishes.**
   The Substack share buttons help, but a personal post on each network
   — even two sentences, "new letter is up, here's what it's about, link
   in the comments" — consistently doubles the open rate of the next
   issue.

That's it. The hard part is the writing. The rest of this file is the
writing.

## Publication Setup

### Suggested publication name

**The Rulio Method**

I considered three names: "The Rulio Method," "5 Minutes," and "Notes
from Brussels." I ruled out "Notes from Brussels" because it's vague
and doesn't help anyone decide whether to subscribe. "5 Minutes" is
great and is the working title of the lead magnet — but as a
publication name it reads like a productivity newsletter, not a
wellness-adjacent one.

"The Rulio Method" wins because it does three things at once: it
tells the reader it's about a specific approach, it implies there *is*
a method (so there's something to learn), and it carries the brand
name for free. The word "method" also lets you go wider later — you
don't have to write about frequencies every issue.

If you want a fallback: "Five Minutes" with a tagline that names
solfeggio.

### Tagline (1 sentence, 60 chars max)

**A founder's notes on focus, sound, and what really works.**

Character count: 58. Reads as honest, scoped, and a little
vulnerable (the "really works" is the hook — it implies that
other people are lying, which is true in this category).

### Bio (150 chars max)

**Solfeggio, focus, and the slow build of Rulio. From Roel in Brussels. No fluff, no miracle claims, no funnels in your inbox.**

Character count: 137. Sets expectations: founder, location, the
"solfeggio" keyword for search/discovery, and three small promises
(no fluff, no miracles, no spam) that match the voice.

## Issue 1: Launch post

**Title:** The 5-Minute Solfeggio Protocol

**Subtitle / dek (optional, Substack has a field for this):**
Nine frequencies, one timer, and the small ritual that brought me back
from burnout.

**Estimated read time:** 5 min

**Body:**

I burned out the slow way. Then a 528 Hz tone, five minutes a day, accidentally put me back together.

I burned out the slow way. Not the dramatic way — not the
crying-in-a-bathroom way, not the "I'm fine, everything's
fine" way you read about in essays by people who've already
recovered. I burned out the way a lot of founders and
knowledge workers burn out: I stopped noticing. I stopped
noticing that I'd stopped sleeping. I stopped noticing that
I was answering emails at 1am and calling it discipline. I
stopped noticing that the Sunday-night dread had been there
for eighteen months and was no longer even registering as a
feeling. The last thing I noticed was that I noticed
nothing.

A friend — a sound engineer, of all people — said, "I think
you need a frequency." He sent me a 528 Hz sine wave, five
minutes long, and told me to put it on after my morning
coffee and not to do anything else. Not check email. Not
meditate. Not journal. Just sit. I did it for a week. I did
not expect anything. I am writing this letter from the
position of someone who, against his own preferences, was
helped by a 5-minute tone.

I am not going to tell you that a 528 Hz sine wave cured my
burnout. That would be a lie, and a stupid one. I am going to
tell you that it gave me a five-minute daily container — a
thing I could do, at the same time, in the same chair, with
no inputs — and that the container turned out to be the
medicine. The frequency was the door. The five minutes was
the room.

That is what the Rulio protocol is. Nine solfeggio
frequencies in 5-minute sessions, with one rule: do it at
the same time every day, sitting down, eyes closed, and do
not do anything else. The session auto-stops at 5:00. There
is no streak counter, no achievement, no social feed. There
is one button. The button plays a tone. The tone ends. You
stand up.

The protocol doesn't tell you which frequency to use. That choice is
yours, and it's the part where you get to pay attention to yourself
instead of outsourcing it to an algorithm. Below are the nine
frequencies, what each one is traditionally associated with, and the
one I'd try first if you're not sure. Pick one that matches how you
feel right now, not the one you think you "should" pick. The whole
point is the small act of honest self-check.

**174 Hz** — the "foundation" tone. Traditional use: physical comfort,
grounding, the feeling of a body in a chair. Start here if you
have been on your feet all day, or clenched for hours, or cannot
feel your feet. The use case is the one a physiotherapist would
recognise: bringing awareness back into the body before doing
anything else.

**285 Hz** — the "recovery" tone. Traditional use: tissue recovery,
the cellular layer of rest. The use case is the post-illness day,
the day after a hard workout, the morning after a sleepless night
when your body feels like it owes you something. I don't have
strong opinions on 285 because I haven't needed it often, but the
people who swear by solfeggio swear loudest about this one.

**396 Hz** — the "release" tone. Traditional use: releasing fear,
undoing guilt, the lower-gut work of letting go. The use case is
the Sunday-night dread. If you recognise the dread — that
specific feeling of a week starting you didn't agree to — play
396 on Sunday evening and sit with it for five minutes. This is
the one I send to friends first.

**417 Hz** — the "change" tone. Traditional use: undoing patterns,
breaking the small loops (the 2am scroll, the third coffee, the
email you rewrite five times before sending). The use case is
when you know what you want to change but the wanting has stopped
moving the needle. 417 doesn't do the changing. It gives you a
five-minute window where the change feels possible.

**528 Hz** — the "transformation" tone, and the one most people
have heard of. Traditional use: repair, connection, the heart.
The use case is the day you're grieving something, the morning
you wake up and something is heavier than it should be. 528 is
also the one I default to when I have no reason to choose
anything specific, and it's the one I started with.

**639 Hz** — the "connection" tone. Traditional use:
relationships, the work of being with other people on purpose.
The use case is the day before a hard conversation, or the day
after one that didn't go well. It is also the one a lot of
couples listen to together, which I mention only because it's
true and people search for it.

**741 Hz** — the "expression" tone. Traditional use: problem-
solving, speaking your truth, the throat-chakra-adjacent
idea of saying the thing you actually mean. The use case is
the blank-page problem — the proposal you can't start, the
message you can't draft, the thing that needs to come out and
won't. I use 741 when I'm stuck on a piece of writing. It is
reliably five minutes of not-doing-anything-else, which is
usually enough to unstick the next sentence.

**852 Hz** — the "intuition" tone. Traditional use: returning to
spiritual order, trusting what you already know. The use case
is decision fatigue — you've been second-guessing for too long
and the right answer is in the building. 852 is for sitting
with a decision you already quietly made. It is not a
decision-making tool. It is a decision-confirming tool. Use
accordingly.

**963 Hz** — the "oneness" tone. Traditional use: connection to
something larger than the small self, the highest of the
solfeggio set. The use case is the existential afternoon — the
one where everything is fine and nothing is meaningful. I use
963 rarely because the use case is rare. When I need it I
need it a lot.

That's the set. Nine tones, one timer, one rule. Pick the one that
matches today. Not yesterday. Not tomorrow. Today.

How to start. Pick one frequency. Not the one you've heard of.
Not the one your friend recommended. The one whose paragraph
above you read and thought, "yeah, that one." Set a timer for
five minutes. Sit. Press play. Close your eyes. When the
session ends, stand up. Do this for seven days in a row, same
time, same place. After seven days you'll know two things:
whether the practice is for you, and which frequency your
body actually asks for. That's the whole protocol. Everything
else on rulio.app is in service of those five minutes.

**— Roel**

If you try the protocol, reply to this email with what you noticed.
I read every one. — Roel

## Issue 2: Behind the scenes

**Title:** Why I built Rulio (and what I got wrong)

**Subtitle / dek:**
Three mistakes, three things I'd do differently, and an open
invitation to compare notes.

**Estimated read time:** 6 min

**Body:**

Last week I sent out the first issue of this letter. A few of you
wrote back asking the question I was hoping you'd ask: "Why did
you build this thing?" I've been avoiding the honest version of
that answer for about a year. Here it is. I built Rulio because
a 528 Hz sine wave helped me through a bad stretch, and I thought
— naively, then arrogantly, then stubbornly — that I could put
the help inside a small app. Most of the build was fine. Some
of it was not. This is the not part.

I am going to tell you three mistakes I made. I am not going to
soften them. I am not going to end each one with "and that's why
it worked out." It did not always work out. Some of the things
I did cost me time, money, and a friendship. The reason I'm
telling you is not so you can admire the lessons. The reason
I'm telling you is so that if you are building something, you
might skip the part I had to live through.

**Mistake 1: I spent four months on the brand before I shipped
the product.** What I did: I hired a designer. I iterated on
the logo. I picked a colour palette. I wrote the landing-page
copy three times. I built a brand book. I did not, during
those four months, ship a single line of the actual app. What
I should have done: shipped a one-page site with a single
button that played a single tone, and asked ten people to use
it for a week. What I learned: a brand is a story you tell
about a product that exists. I had the story first, the
product second, and for four months the story was for nobody,
because there was no product to tell it about. I now ship
ugly. I ship ugly, then I ship a small improvement, then I
ship another small improvement. I do not ship a brand book.

**Mistake 2: I optimised for a paying user before I had a free
user.** What I did: I built the Pro tier — subscription
management, paywall, dunning, the works — and I did it before
I had a single person using the free tier. I had the
courage of a Stripe integration and the cowardice of a
landing page. What I should have done: made the free tier
genuinely free, with no card field and no countdown timer
and no email gate. I had to be talked into this by a friend
who said, "Roel, you have fourteen audio files and a logo.
You don't have a paywall problem. You have a 'do people
even want this' problem." She was right. What I learned:
the most expensive thing in a small product is not the
engineering. It is the distance between you and a real
human being who has tried the thing and told you what they
thought. Everything I built before I had that was a
founder's daydream with a domain name attached.

**Mistake 3: I treated the solfeggio category like a
"wellness" category.** What I did: I wrote copy that
sounded like the other apps in the category. I used the
words "healing" and "transformation" and "vibration." I
ran the colours towards the pastel end of the spectrum.
I used the same hero illustration every wellness app uses.
What I should have done: written copy that sounded like
me, which is to say: a little too direct, a little too
specific, and a little allergic to the word "vibration."
What I learned: the category attracts a certain kind of
buyer, and I was not building for that buyer. I was
building for the person I was when I burned out — a
skeptic, a knowledge worker, someone who has been burned
by miracle claims before and would click off the moment
they saw the word "manifest." So I rewrote everything.
The new copy is closer to this letter: honest, specific,
willing to say "I don't know" out loud. I lost a chunk
of the wellness-audience traffic when I did. I do not
care. I would rather have a hundred readers who trust
me than ten thousand who don't.

So those are the three mistakes. If you read this far and you
are a builder, you probably have a matching list. The honest
version of "what I got wrong" is mostly the same for everyone
who has ever shipped anything: ship too late, monetise too
early, sound like the people around you for too long. The
specifics differ. The pattern doesn't.

Three things I'd do differently, if I were starting Rulio
again from zero with what I know now. One: I would write the
newsletter first and the app second. The newsletter is where
I learned what people actually wanted. It is also where I
learned that most of my product opinions were wrong. Two: I
would talk to ten users before writing a single line of
code, and I would not let any of them be friends. Friends
tell you what you want to hear. Strangers tell you what
you need to know. Three: I would set a hard cap on the
number of features in v1 — say, three — and I would not
ship until those three were boringly good. The biggest
threat to a small product is not the competitor. It is
the founder's enthusiasm. Every new idea is a new half-built
thing. Half-built things are why most small products die.

That's the whole confession. I'm not going to wrap it up with
a tidy bow, because the project isn't tidy and I'm not done
making mistakes. I'm just done making *those* mistakes. The
next ones are different and probably worse.

One ask. If you are building something — an app, a product,
a newsletter, a small service — and you want to compare
notes, my DMs are open. I read every message. I will not
charge you for the call. I will not put you on a sales
call. I will not ask for your email list. I will just talk
to you for thirty minutes and tell you the things I wish
someone had told me. If you have ever felt like the only
person in the world building a small product, you are not,
and the fastest way to feel less alone in it is to talk to
someone else who is. Use the door.

**— Roel**

If you're building something and want to compare notes, my DMs
are open. — Roel

## Issue 3: The science

**Title:** What solfeggio frequencies actually do (according to
research)

**Subtitle / dek:**
A long, careful letter about nine tones, one retracted Stanford
paper, and what the literature will and will not let us say.

**Estimated read time:** 8 min

**Body:**

This is the longest letter I will send for a while. It is
also the one I have been avoiding, because the honest
version of "What does the science say about solfeggio
frequencies?" requires me to say four different things in
the right order. I am going to try.

Solfeggio frequencies are a set of nine tones — 174, 285, 396,
417, 528, 639, 741, 852, and 963 Hz — used in a particular
lineage of Western esotericism and, more recently, in the
broader wellness and "sound healing" categories. The set is
not ancient in the way a lot of marketing copy claims. The
most-cited attribution — that they come from a sixth-century
hymn to John the Baptist — was traced by musicologist Willi
Apffelstaedt in a 2023 study to a much later source, and the
connection is contested. The first honest thing to say about
solfeggio is that the historical claim is shakier than the
copy suggests.

The second thing to say is that the *musical* claim — that
these specific frequencies have a relationship to a six-
interval scale that pre-dates the twelve-tone equal
temperament system — is plausible. The Pythagorean scale that
the solfeggio set echoes is real, was used in medieval church
music, and has a genuine mathematical structure. The claim
that the modern solfeggio set uses the *same* frequencies the
medieval church used, in a continuous unbroken line, is the
part that does not hold up. The frequencies are old. The set
is recent.

The third thing to say is that none of the above means
solfeggio doesn't work, and none means it does. The
*origins* story is contested, and you should be wary of
anyone who leans on it to sell you a result. The
frequencies are audible tones in the audible range. They
will produce a sound. What that sound does in the body
and brain is a separate question, and a much harder one.
That question is what the rest of this letter is about.

So. The nine frequencies and what they are traditionally
used for. I am going to be careful here. "Traditionally"
means "in the modern solfeggio literature, which is itself
a 20th- and 21st-century construction." I am not citing
anything older.

**174 Hz** — Traditional association: physical foundation,
the body's baseline. The use case in the literature is
"felt sense of the body," which is useful because it
doesn't claim a mechanism — just that people report
feeling more in their body after listening.

**285 Hz** — Traditional association: cellular recovery,
rebuilding of tissue. The literature is thin here. This
is one of the two frequencies in the set (the other
being 963) where the traditional claims are the most
mechanism-heavy and the modern research is the most
sparse.

**396 Hz** — Traditional association: release of fear
and guilt. The use case is emotional unloading. This
is the one I send to friends first because almost
everyone can name a Sunday-night feeling they would
like less of, and the claim is small enough to be
testable by you, which is the right test.

**417 Hz** — Traditional association: undoing patterns,
facilitating change. The use case is breaking small
behavioural loops. I use this when I'm stuck in a
re-write loop. I have no evidence the frequency is
doing anything; I have evidence that sitting still for
five minutes is helping me. I don't need the frequency
to be the active ingredient for the practice to be
useful.

**528 Hz** — Traditional association: transformation,
"repair" (often described in DNA-repair terms in the
literature, the most contested claim in the set), the
heart. The most-heard-of frequency, and the one the
most extravagant claims attach to. I started with 528
because my friend told me to. I keep coming back
because it works for me. Those are two different
things.

**639 Hz** — Traditional association: relationships,
communication, connection. The use case is the work
of being with another person on purpose. It is the
most common frequency in the couples-and-solfeggio
content online, which is a sociological fact rather
than a scientific one.

**741 Hz** — Traditional association: problem-solving,
expression, the throat. The use case is the blank-page
or stuck-feeling problem. The mechanism claim is the
weakest of the set; the use case is the strongest. I
use 741 the way other people use a walk-around-the-
block: it unsticks the next thing.

**852 Hz** — Traditional association: intuition,
returning to spiritual order. The use case is decision
confirmation. It pairs well with a journal entry —
write down what you noticed during the five minutes
and revisit it a week later. You have a record of your
own decision-making, which is better than a frequency.

**963 Hz** — Traditional association: connection to
something larger, the "oneness" frequency. The use
case is the existential afternoon. The most
metaphysical of the nine. I use it least because my
life has very few existential afternoons, and when
it does I have other things for those.

That is the catalogue. Now the harder part. What does the
research actually show?

The research picture is mixed, and the mixed-ness is
instructive. There are three honest things to say
about it, and I want to say them in order.

First, there is a small body of work on specific
frequencies — including some in the solfeggio set —
showing modest, replicable effects on measurable things:
heart-rate variability, perceived stress scores, certain
markers of autonomic nervous system activity. The caveats
are: most of these studies are small (under 50
participants), many are not blinded, and almost none of
them isolate the *frequency* from the *act of sitting
still for five minutes with your eyes closed*. That last
caveat matters. Five minutes of quiet sitting is a known
intervention. It lowers heart rate, shifts the autonomic
balance, and is genuinely helpful for a lot of people.
Any study that compares "528 Hz for 5 minutes" against
"silence for 5 minutes" is testing two interventions,
not one.

Second, there is a much larger body of marketing
copy, podcasts, and YouTube videos that cite a
small number of studies as if those studies proved
much more than they did. A study finds a small
effect under specific conditions, the study gets
cited as if it found a large effect under all
conditions, and the resulting claim ends up in a
Substack ad for someone else's product. The honest
move is to read the study. Most people do not. I
did not, for the first year, and I am slightly
embarrassed about that now.

Third — the example I promised in the welcome email
— there was a 2018 paper out of a Stanford-affiliated
lab that got a lot of press for claiming a specific
frequency had a measurable biological effect on human
cells. The paper was later retracted. The retraction
notice cited problems with the data and methodology. I
had cited the original paper in conversations more times
than I had read it. I no longer cite it. The pattern —
a small lab result, a big press cycle, a slow
retraction, a long tail of citations that never update
— is the most common shape "science proves it" claims
take in this category. I want to name it so you can
recognise it elsewhere.

What that means for the protocol is two things,
and they are the only two I want you to take away
from this letter.

One: the practice is the intervention. The
frequency is a useful pretext for the practice,
and for some people it is more than a pretext.
If 396 Hz reliably helps you with the Sunday-
night dread, that is real — in the way a hot
shower or a walk in the park is real. I am
asking you to be honest with yourself about
whether the *specific frequency* is doing the
work, or whether the *act of stopping for five
minutes* is doing the work. The second answer is
the more useful one. The first locks you into a
specific app and a specific set of tones. The
second frees you.

Two: be careful what you read. The wellness category
is full of citations to studies that either don't
exist, were retracted, or were saying something much
smaller than the citation implies. The retracted
Stanford paper is not unique — it is the rule. If a
claim about solfeggio has the shape "Study proves
that [tone] does [miraculous thing]," find the study
and read it. If you cannot find the study, that is
the answer. If the study does not say what the claim
says it says, that is also the answer. The point of
this letter is to make you read the study, not to
make you trust me.

If you read all three and you got this far, you are now
more literate on this topic than 95% of the people
selling this category.

**— Roel**

## Appendix C: Image specs (if you add a header image to each issue)

Substack renders the post header image at roughly 1456 × 180 px
desktop and a square crop on mobile. A safe image is 1600 × 1000
px (16:10), exported as a JPEG under 250 KB, with the title
text in the lower third (so it doesn't get covered by the
post-title overlay Substack adds on mobile).

If you don't want to design anything, the cheapest acceptable
option is: a single frequency number in the brand typeface
("528") on the cosmic-black (#0c0c0e) background, the
electric-blue (#5BB8FF) accent, no other elements. That
image alone, used three times, is a publication. Do not
over-design this.

## Appendix E: The five things to never do in this newsletter

These are written for future-you, the one who will be
six issues in and wondering whether to start a "premium

and here is why.

   you start using AI to draft the body, the day you
   start using a ghostwriter, the day you start using
   a VA to clean up your sentences — that is the day
   the newsletter becomes a feed. Feeds are not
   newsletters. Newsletters are people.

## Appendix H: A note on the word "method"

The word method is doing a lot of work in the publication
name. It is not, in the strict sense, a method. A method
implies a peer-reviewed, replicable, falsifiable procedure.
The Rulio protocol is none of those things. It is a
small personal practice that I find useful and that
other people report finding useful. Calling it a method
is a useful fiction — it tells the reader there is
something to learn, and that the something has a shape.

The honest move is to keep the word in the publication
name (because it works) and to use the rest of the
newsletter to gently push back on the implication. The
publication is called The Rulio Method. The body of
the publication is, increasingly, "and what the method
actually is, is smaller and weirder than the name
suggests." That tension — the small honest thing inside
the larger name — is, I think, the most useful shape a
newsletter can have. It is also, not coincidentally, the
shape of Rulio itself.

## Appendix K: Reply-handling playbook

You said in the footers that you read every reply. The
footers also invite the reader to reply. This appendix
is how to handle the replies when they come in, which
they will, in numbers higher than you expect, and with
more emotional weight than you expect. The replies are
the most useful data you will get from the publication.
Treat them accordingly.

**The four kinds of replies you will get, and what to do
with each:**

1. **"I tried it and it worked."** This is the most
   common. The reply is short. The reply is genuine.
   Reply within 24 hours. Ask one follow-up question —
   "what did you notice first?" — and let the
   conversation continue if it wants to. Do not pitch.
   Do not promote. Do not put them on a list. The
   reply is the relationship. If the relationship
   wants to grow, it will tell you.

2. **"I tried it and it didn't work."** This is rarer
   and more useful. The reply is usually longer. Read
   it twice. The honest reply here is "thank you for
   telling me, can I ask one thing" and then a single
   specific question that helps you understand what
   "didn't work" means — the frequency, the time of
   day, the chair, the headphones, the five minutes,
   or the practice itself. The people who tell you
   "it didn't work" are the ones whose feedback will
   make the next version of the protocol better. They
   are doing free QA for you. Treat them as such.

3. **"I have a question about the science."** This is
   the reply that will arrive in the largest numbers
   after Issue 3. The reader has read all the way
   through and now has a specific question, usually
   something like "what about the 440 Hz tuning
   argument" or "did anyone study 396 Hz specifically."
   The honest answer to most of these is "I don't
   know" or "I read one study that suggested X, here
   is the link." Link to the paper. Do not summarise
   the paper. Let the reader read the paper. That is
   the whole point of the publication.

4. **"I want to write about this, can I interview
   you."** This reply is rarer. It is also the most
   time-consuming. The honest answer is "yes, and
   here are the three things I will not talk about."
   Name the off-limits things up front. The
   conversation will be better for it.

**One rule for all four:** reply within 24 hours on
weekdays, within 48 hours on weekends. If you can't
reply in that window, send a one-line "got it, will
reply properly by Thursday" so the person knows the
message landed. The single most damaging thing you
can do to a small newsletter is to invite a reply
and not respond. You will not be forgiven for it,
and you will not be told. People will just quietly
unsubscribe.

## Appendix N: Reader personas (the 4 kinds of readers)

After the first 30 days you will start to see patterns
in who reads the publication. The patterns are useful
because they tell you who you are actually writing
for. There are four personas you will recognise.

1. **The skeptic-founder.** A founder or knowledge
   worker who has been burned by wellness marketing
   before. Reads every issue. Replies with a question
   or a pushback. Will never share the publication
   publicly, because the persona's whole identity is
   "I do not share wellness things." Will refer a
   friend in a DM. Treat this persona as your
   quality-control filter. Their pushback is the
   signal that you have not lost the plot.

2. **The wellness-curious.** A person who is
   genuinely interested in solfeggio, sound, or
   meditation adjacent practices. Reads the
   publication, occasionally shares it on social.
   Will sometimes ask the science questions. Will
   sometimes try the protocol. Will sometimes
   unsubscribe. The unsubscribe is fine. This is
   the highest-churn persona, but also the
   highest-reach persona.

3. **The lurker.** A person who reads every issue
   but never replies, never shares, never
   unsubscribes. Will be on your list for years.
   Will be the largest single segment of your
   audience. You will never know who they are or
   what they took from the publication. That is
   fine. Write for them by writing the best
   letter you can, and trust that the work is
   doing its work.

4. **The freeloader-with-a-pitch.** A person who
   subscribed to get the lead magnet, or the
   ebook, or the discount code, and who is now
   on your list. Will open the first email and
   maybe the second. Will then go cold. The cold
   is fine. The list will accumulate a
   meaningful percentage of these, and they are
   the least useful segment. Don't optimise for
   them. Don't write to them. They will leave
   on their own.

The 4-persona map is a tool for noticing, not for
targeting. The temptation is to write different
emails for each persona. Resist. Write one email,
well. The personas will each get something out of
it on their own terms.

## Appendix O: When to introduce paid

Don't. Not yet. Here is the rule I would use, and the
reason behind it.

The rule: do not introduce the paid tier until the free
list is at least 1,000 subscribers AND you have shipped
at least 12 issues AND the open rate is above 35%.

The reason: the paid tier is a friction layer. It
splits the audience into "the people who pay" and "the
people who don't," and the second group quietly
resents the first group, and the first group quietly
expects things, and the second group quietly leaves.
The publication was not designed to be split. It was
designed to be one letter, written honestly, sent to
people who want to read it. The paid tier changes the
incentive in ways that are not obvious until you are
already inside them.

When you do introduce the paid tier, do it for a
single reason: the free list has grown to a size
where the marginal cost of an additional subscriber
is real (mostly your time replying to emails) and the
marginal benefit of a paid subscriber covers it. Not
before.

The first paid tier, if you ever run one, should be
one of these three shapes:

- **A "supporters" tier at €5/mo or €50/yr.** No
  extra content. No second newsletter. Just a button
  that says "I want this to keep existing." The
  supporters will be your most loyal readers and your
  smallest segment. That is fine.
- **A "sounding board" tier at €19/mo.** A monthly
  group call, capped at 12 people, for readers who
  want to talk about the practice. The call is not
  a sales call. The call is not a coaching offer.
  The call is a place to compare notes with other
  people doing the practice. If the call gets too
  popular, raise the price. If the call stops being
  useful, kill it.
- **A "second tier" at €9/mo** that gets an extra
  issue per month. The extra issue is the kind of
  thing you would write for yourself. The free list
  is the kind of thing you would write for the
  reader. Both are honest. They are different
  audiences, and that's fine.

The worst version of a paid tier is the one where
you move the best content behind the paywall. The
moment the best content is gated, the publication
is no longer the publication. It is a content
business with a freebie attached. Different thing.
Different incentives. Different outcome.

## Appendix P: SEO and discoverability on Substack

Substack has a search function and a recommendations
feed. The publication will be indexed by Google within
a few days of the first issue going out. A few
discoverability notes.

**Tags and categories:** Substack lets you tag your
publication. Use the most specific tags you can. For
The Rulio Method, the right tags are: "wellness,"
"focus,""mindfulness,""solfeggio,""frequency,""sound
healing,""founder journal,""Brussels." Don't use tags
you wouldn't want to be associated with. Substack
shows related publications under each post, and the
algorithm puts you next to the other publications
with the same tags. The neighbourhood is a real
neighbourhood.

**The about page:** Substack has an "About" page for
each publication. The text on that page is the
single most-cited piece of writing on the publication
(it shows up in search results, in the publication
directory, and in the footer of every email). The
bio you put in this file is the about-page text. Keep
it short, honest, and searchable. Don't pad it with
keywords. The reader will read it once and decide
whether to subscribe. The Google crawler will read
it a thousand times. Write for the reader.

**The first paragraph of every post:** Substack
indexes the first paragraph. The first paragraph of
each issue should contain the publication's main
keywords at least once. "Solfeggio" should appear
in the first paragraph of every issue. "Five
minutes" should appear in the first paragraph of
most issues. The reader will not notice this. The
search engine will. The trick is to write a first
paragraph that contains the keywords AND is also
the most compelling sentence you can write. That is
the constraint. It is a real constraint, and it is
not as hard as it sounds.

**The URL slug:** Substack lets you customise the URL
slug for each post. Use the publication's name + a
short, hyphen-separated version of the title. The
slug is permanent. The title can change. Don't put
keywords in the slug that you wouldn't want to be
associated with for the next five years.

## Appendix Q: A short note on the welcome email length

The welcome email in this file is 153 words. That is
deliberately long for a welcome email. Most welcome
emails are 30-50 words. The longer version is the
right call for a publication like this one for three
reasons.

1. The welcome email is the highest-converting email
   you will ever send. A subscriber has just typed
   their email into a box. They are at peak
   attention. The next 200 words of their inbox
   experience is the most valuable real estate you
   have. Use it.
2. The subscriber who reads the welcome email is
   three times more likely to open Issue 1. The
   subscriber who reads the welcome email AND
   clicks the link is five times more likely to
   become a long-term reader. The welcome email is
   the on-ramp. The on-ramp needs to do its work.
3. A welcome email that is too short feels
   transactional. A welcome email that is too long
   feels like a sales email. 150 words is the sweet
   spot — long enough to be personal, short enough
   to be read in one sitting. The PS line is
   allowed to be a soft CTA. The body is not.

If you write a welcome email and it feels
overlong, you are probably right. Trim it. If it
feels short, you are probably wrong. Add one more
paragraph. The difference between a 100-word
welcome email and a 150-word welcome email is the
difference between a subscriber and a reader.

## Appendix R: What to do if an issue goes viral

This is a problem you would like to have. Here is the
playbook for the day it happens.

**The first hour:**

- Don't tweet about it. Don't write a Note. Don't
  check the open rate. The thing is happening on its
  own. Let it happen.
- Reply to every email. The viral day is also the
  day with the highest reply rate, because the
  new readers are the most curious.

**The first day:**

- Don't change the cadence. Don't publish an
  "emergency" follow-up. Don't post a "wow, this
  went viral" Note. The readers who found you
  through the viral post will subscribe because
  of the viral post, not because of an immediate
  follow-up. Let them land on the publication
  as it is. The publication is the product.
- Pin the welcome email. Make sure the
  auto-send is working. The new subscribers are
  arriving faster than the welcome email can
  send, and a missed welcome email is a missed
  first impression.

**The first week:**

- Don't change the schedule. Issue 2 is going
  out on day 7. Issue 2 will get a much higher
  signup rate than normal, and it will get a
  much higher open rate than normal. The
  Tuesday-after-viral-post is the single most
  valuable real estate in the publication's
  calendar. Use it.

**The second week:**

- Don't panic when the open rate drops. The
  viral subscribers open Issue 1 and Issue 2
  and then go cold. The cold is normal. The
  open rate is not the metric that matters.
  The metric that matters is the number of
  subscribers who are still opening Issue 6,
  Issue 8, Issue 12. The viral bump is a
  long-tail event, not a short-term spike.

**The single most useful thing to do on the
viral day:** write down three things. (a) What
the viral post said. (b) Where it was shared
from. (c) What the new subscribers have in
common with the existing subscribers. (a) and
(b) you can do from the data. (c) you can only
do by reading replies. (c) is the most
valuable of the three.

## Appendix S: What to do if an issue flops

This is a problem you will have. Here is the
playbook for the day it happens.

**The first day:**

- Don't apologise. Don't tweet "sorry this
  issue wasn't as good." Don't write a Note
  about it. The issue is out. People are
  reading it. Some of them are not. The
  readers who are not reading it are not
  going to start reading it because you
  apologised for the issue.
- Do read the replies. The flop-day
  replies are different from the good-day
  replies. They are shorter. They are more
  polite. They are less likely to engage
  with the content. That is the signal.
  The signal is not "this issue was bad."
  The signal is "this issue was not for
  this reader." The two things are
  different.

**The first week:**

- Look at the open rate. If the open rate
  for the flopped issue is below 50% of the
  median open rate for the publication, the
  issue did not land. The cause is usually
  one of three things: the title didn't
  hook, the first paragraph didn't carry,
  or the topic wasn't the topic the reader
  signed up for. All three are fixable in
  the next issue.
- Write the next issue anyway. The biweekly
  cadence is the brand. A flopped issue
  followed by a skipped issue is the worst
  possible outcome. A flopped issue
  followed by a good issue is a normal
  outcome. The cadence survives the flop.
  The trust survives the cadence.

**The first month:**

- Look at the long-term open rate. The
  flopped issue will pull down the average
  for one cycle. The average will recover
  by Issue 4 if the next two issues are
  at the normal quality bar. The long-
  term open rate is the metric that
  matters. The flopped issue is noise.

**The most useful thing to do on the flop
day:** write down what you would change
about the issue if you could write it
again. Not what the readers would change.
What you would change. The flopped issue
is the issue that will teach you the most
about your own writing. The lesson will
be uncomfortable. The lesson will also
be the most useful thing in your writing
practice for the next six months. The
flop is a gift. The gift is in the
discomfort.

   impressions of a 5-tweet thread. The thread
   also lets you quote the best paragraph from the
   issue, which is the single most shareable unit
   in the whole publication.
4. **Post on LinkedIn with a personal frame, not
   a marketing frame.** "I wrote about X because
   Y happened to me last week" gets 5x the
   impressions of "Issue 2 of my newsletter is
   up." The personal frame is the only frame that
   works on LinkedIn for a one-person publication.
5. **Send three personal DMs to three specific
   people who would actually read the issue.** Not
   a generic blast. Three specific DMs, each
   referencing a specific paragraph from the
   issue. The DMs are the highest-leverage
   distribution channel you have. They are also
   the most time-consuming. Spend the time.

## Appendix U: A note on the emoji rule

The voice rule in this file is "no emoji in the body."
The welcome email footer is the one place where
emoji is allowed. The reason for the rule is that
the publication is going for the "long letter to a
friend" voice, and the friend would not be sending
emoji in a 1,200-word letter about their burnout.
The voice is consistent, and the voice is the
brand.

The exception is the welcome email PS, where one
small flourish is allowed if it fits. A single
small symbol — a plant, a coffee cup, a leaf. One
symbol, not two, not three. The welcome email is
the door. The door is allowed one small flourish.
The body of the letter is not the door. The body
of the letter is the room. The room is plain.

If you find yourself wanting to add a flourish to
the body of an issue, that is a signal that the
writing is leaning on the flourish for emotional
work that the writing should be doing itself. The
writing is the work. The flourish is not.

## Appendix V: A note on writing the next issue

You will, at some point, sit down to write Issue 4
and not know what to write. The trick I would
suggest is to write the reply to a reader's
email, and then make the reply longer.

The reason this works is that the reader's email
is the actual question the publication exists to
answer. The reader who replies with "what about
people who meditate" or "what does the science
say about binaural beats" is giving you the
title of the next issue, the subtitle, and the
first sentence. The reply is the test. The
issue is the answer.

A 200-word reply can be expanded into a 1,200-
word issue by adding: a personal story, a
counter-argument, a small data point, and a
clear "here is what I would do" closer. Those
four things are the structure of every issue
in this publication. Once you have the four
things, the issue writes itself.

The publication that runs out of ideas is the
publication that stopped reading the replies.
The replies are the only signal you have that
the publication is connecting. Don't stop
reading the replies.

## Appendix W: A note on the publication's relationship to Rulio

The publication is a separate thing from the
app, and the separation is the point. The
publication is for readers. The app is for
users. The two have different incentives, and
the two should not be conflated.

The publication is allowed to:

- Link to the app once per issue, in the
  footer, with the exact CTA from the spec
  for each issue.
- Quote a paragraph from the app's about page
  if it adds to the letter.
- Reference a feature of the app by name
  ("the 25-minute focus session" or "the
  5-minute Qi session") if the reference
  serves the letter.

The publication is not allowed to:

- Run a discount code in the body of an issue.
- Offer a "subscribers only" feature in the
  app.
- Pitch a paid product in the first 200
  words of an issue.
- Use the publication as a sales channel for
  a launch or a partnership.

The line is the same line that runs through
the rest of the launch kit: the publication
is for the reader. The reader is not the
target. The reader is the audience. The
audience is the product. The product is the
reader. The product is the letter. The
letter is the product.
